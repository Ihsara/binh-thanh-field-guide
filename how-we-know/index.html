How we know

This atlas reads frozen extracts, never a live map. Each page uses one release of its data and never mixes two, with three exceptions. What the Map Can't See sets the volunteer map's latest year against one commercial download, because the gap between them is its subject. Before the Towers and The Open Peninsula set the old military sheet beside dates from our history ledger. Every number you see is computed from a file in this repository, and the table for each map below names that file, the release it comes from and how the number was counted.

Two sources feed the maps of the first chapter. Overture Maps is a commercial-grade open dataset that merges several providers into one list of places, with a category for each. OpenStreetMap is a map drawn by volunteers: streets, wards, rivers and the buildings between them. Neither is a census, and both miss the stall that has no sign. The last map of that chapter says what that costs. Place data: Overture Maps Foundation, CDLA-Permissive-2.0. Streets and boundaries: © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

What It Used to Be. The second chapter sets old maps under today's streets. Its main map is the 1984 topographic sheet of the US Defense Mapping Agency, a US federal work in the public domain; the scan is from the University of Texas Libraries. In this part of the city the sheet's drawing does not sit where its own printed grid says it should, missing it by up to 116.8 m. So we registered the sheet to what it draws instead: 25 road junctions and bridge mid-spans, each matched to the same place in OpenStreetMap. Every one of those points was scored by a fit that never saw it, and the median miss is 22.6 m.

The sheet ends before the district does. It holds 94.3% of the district and 81.4% of Phường Bình Quới; the eastern tip of the Thanh Đa peninsula is off its edge, and every share in the chapter is of the part on the sheet. The built-up fill, printed red on the sheet and traced in teal on the maps, is found by its colour, checked against squares we labelled by hand. Every colour setting that reads those squares equally well gives a district share between 15.1% and 19.1%, so the headline is a rough share within that band. No page gives a built-up share for today: only the old sheet is measured.

The French survey of 1895 (Joly, Plan des environs de Saïgon) is public domain (copyright expired), via Wikimedia Commons. The fit it was given before misses its check points by a median of 1,218 m, too far to draw under today's streets, so it is shown as a picture. The inset on the road page is Trần Văn Học's map of Gia Định of 1815, photographed from a later printed reproduction (Địa chí Văn hóa Thành phố Hồ Chí Minh), via Wikimedia Commons, where the file is tagged public domain in the US. Credit: Trần Văn Học, Bản đồ Gia Định; photograph uploaded by Bùi Thụy Đào Nguyên (file page). It is illustrative only and is never laid on the map. The line of the Mandarin Road, and the dates on the tower and peninsula pages, come from the sister site's research: the route follows the streets historians name, and each date has two independent sources.
